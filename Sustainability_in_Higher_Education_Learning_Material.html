Welcome to the course

About This Learning Material

Higher education sits at a crossroads of every sustainability question our societies face. Universities prepare the teachers, engineers, doctors, administrators and entrepreneurs who will run tomorrow’s world; they also produce much of the knowledge those decisions will rest upon. That is why Sustainability in Higher Education is not simply a topic you study — it is a lens through which you will re-examine what teaching, research and institutional life are for.

This learning material covers the complete syllabus of Paper XXI (EDN-21): Sustainability in Higher Education in three units. Unit 1 builds the conceptual foundations: what sustainable development means, for whom, and how the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) travel into higher education through teaching, research, campus operations and governance. Unit 2 shifts to perspective and practice: the role of the university in society, how institutions engage with the SDGs, and the pedagogical innovations that make learning transformative. Unit 3 confronts the hard edges: barriers and problems, the Three Horizons and RADAR frameworks, and the indicators that tell us whether education for sustainable development (ESD) is genuinely happening or merely being announced.

Every unit follows the same rhythm so that studying becomes a habit rather than a hunt. You begin with key terms, move through five fully-developed topics, meet visual organisers (mind maps, flowcharts, tables and charts), and close with a self-check quiz, reflection questions and links to the engagement activities required by the syllabus. Boxes marked “Did You Know?” appear throughout to keep the journey recreational as well as rigorous. By the end, you should be able not only to reproduce the ideas in an examination, but to audit your own institution and classroom with them.

Learning Outcomes

On successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

  • LO1 — Understand sustainability in higher education and explain its origins, definitions and rationale in plain language.
  • LO2 — Describe the SDG goals and the key competencies (systems, anticipatory, normative, strategic, interpersonal) that sustainability learning develops.
  • LO3 — Understand responsible research and innovation in sustainability, including the RADAR qualities of research practice.
  • LO4 — Explore the approaches available to higher education institutions for engaging with the SDGs, from disciplinary redesign to whole-institution strategies.
  • LO5 — Understand the challenges of ESD — curricular, structural and cultural — and evaluate institutional progress using ESD indicators.

Key Terms · Unit 1
Sustainable Development — development meeting present needs without compromising future generations’ ability to meet theirs (Brundtland, 1987).
SDGs — 17 universal goals adopted by all UN member states in 2015 to end poverty, protect the planet and ensure prosperity by 2030.
Agenda 2030 — the UN resolution “Transforming Our World” that carries the 17 goals, 169 targets and the global pledge to leave no one behind.
ESD (Education for Sustainable Development) — learning that empowers people with knowledge, skills, values and agency to shape a sustainable society.
Triple Bottom Line — the three interlocking dimensions of sustainability: people (social), planet (environmental), prosperity (economic).

1.1
Sustainable Development for Who, How, When and to What Effect?

The most quoted sentence in sustainability literature appeared in 1987, when the World Commission on Environment and Development — chaired by Gro Harlem Brundtland — defined sustainable development as “development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.” The sentence looks simple, yet it contains two explosive ideas: the idea of needs, particularly the essential needs of the world’s poor, which deserve overriding priority; and the idea of limits, the recognition that the environment cannot indefinitely absorb current levels of extraction and pollution. Sustainable development, in this reading, is simultaneously a critique of business-as-usual growth and a proposal for a fairer kind of progress.

Because the idea is broad, this course organises it through four probing questions that the syllabus names directly. For who? Sustainability is first a question of equity — between countries, between rich and poor, between genders and communities today (intragenerational equity), and between us and those not yet born (intergenerational equity). Whoever designs a sustainability policy must ask whose needs count and who currently lacks a voice. How? This is the question of means: the “three pillars” or triple bottom line of people, planet and prosperity must be pursued together, because poverty driven by environmental collapse is no development at all, and conservation that starves communities is neither just nor durable. Education itself is one of the most powerful “hows” available, which is why it appears in the goals not once but everywhere.

When? gives the agenda its tempo. The UN Decade of Education for Sustainable Development (2005–2014) normalised ESD in classrooms; the Rio+20 conference of 2012 launched the negotiation that produced Agenda 2030 in 2015; and the Paris Agreement of the same year set climate targets for mid-century. Timeframes discipline action: a 2030 horizon for the goals, 2050 for climate neutrality, and an open-ended obligation to generations ahead. To what effect? finally, is the evaluator’s question. Awareness-raising alone does not change systems; the effect sought is a measurable shift in what learners know, value and — crucially — do. That is why modern ESD talks of action competence and why universities increasingly report their sustainability performance rather than merely declaring intentions.

Did You Know?

The SDGs were adopted on 25 September 2015 by all 193 UN member states — the largest number of countries ever to agree a shared development agenda. Behind the 17 goals sit 169 targets and a global indicator framework of over 230 measures used to track progress.

The three pillars predate the goals: the 1972 Stockholm Conference first placed environment on the world development agenda, and the 1992 Rio Earth Summit adopted Agenda 21, whose chapter 36 was devoted entirely to education.

1.2
SDGs in Higher Education; Education About, For and Through the SDGs

Agenda 2030 is unusual among global compacts: it treats education both as a goal in itself (SDG 4: Quality Education) and as a means for every other goal. Higher education carries a double responsibility here. First, it delivers SDG 4 directly through inclusive access, relevant skills and Target 4.7, which asks all learners to acquire knowledge and skills needed to promote sustainable development, human rights, gender equality, a culture of peace and global citizenship. Second, universities advance the other sixteen goals through graduate expertise, applied research, campus practice and public partnerships — from SDG 3 health training to SDG 7 clean-energy engineering and SDG 13 climate research.

ESD practitioners distinguish three orientations of sustainability education, often summarised as about, for and through. Education about the SDGs builds cognitive understanding: students learn the goals, targets, indicators and the evidence behind them. Education for the SDGs is transformative and values-driven: it cultivates the dispositions, competencies and agency to act, treating learners as future change agents rather than as audiences. Education through the SDGs uses the institution itself as the curriculum: greening campus operations, participatory governance and community partnerships become living lessons in how the goals actually get implemented. A mature programme blends all three — knowledge without agency is inert, while activism without understanding is fragile.

1.3
Conceptualising Sustainability in Key HE Functions

A university is not one thing but a bundle of functions: it teaches, researches, builds, buys, employs, governs and reaches outward. Conceptualising sustainability function by function prevents the common failure mode in which sustainability lives in one environmental-science course and nowhere else. Each function can be asked a simple question — what does it mean to do this sustainably? — and each answer becomes a workstream for institutional change. The table below maps the five widely recognised functions that this course, and the international literature from Cortese (2003) to the IAU global surveys, treat as the skeleton of a sustainable university.

Teaching & CurriculumEvery graduate — not only environmental majors — encounters sustainability concepts, competencies and values in their programmeSDG-mapped course outcomes; interdisciplinary electives; ESD embedded in teaching practice
Research & InnovationResearch agendas address sustainability problems and research itself is conducted responsiblyInterdisciplinary sustainability institutes; Responsible R&I; open, ethical publication
Campus OperationsEnergy, water, waste, food, procurement and mobility minimise ecological footprintRenewable energy contracts; zero-waste canteens; green building standards; campus farms as labs
Community EngagementKnowledge and service flow outward; local problems inform learningCommunity service learning; science shops; adult and extension education on sustainability
Governance & AdministrationPolicy, finance and quality systems institutionalise sustainability rather than leaving it to volunteersSustainability policy and committee; SDG reporting; staff incentives; ethical investment
Table 1.2 — Sustainability reconceptualised across the five core functions of a university.

Two insights follow from the functional view. First, the functions are interdependent: a green campus teaches through practice only if the curriculum notices it, and a sustainability-literate graduate community pushes governance further. Cortese (2003) argued that the university is uniquely able to model sustainability because all its functions are, in effect, visible to its own students every day. Second, the functional view exposes gaps quickly: an institution may publish a glossy sustainability strategy (governance) while its teacher-education curriculum remains untouched (teaching) — exactly the “espoused versus enacted” gap that Lee, Barker and Mouasher (2013) documented in Australian universities’ mission statements. Auditing function by function, as you will do in Engagement Task 1, turns such rhetoric-reality gaps into an improvement agenda.

1.4
Strategies for Integrating Sustainability in Higher Education

Knowing what sustainability touches is easier than knowing how to embed it, so researchers have mapped the how into phased strategies. Lozano (2006), studying universities that had genuinely transformed, found a progression from initiation (an individual or small group champions the idea) through incorporation (sustainability enters courses, research and operations) to institutionalisation (policy, culture and quality systems make the change permanent). Many institutions stall between the second and third phases: a dynamic vice-chancellor launches initiatives, but when leadership changes, sustainability evaporates because it was attached to a person rather than to the institution’s rules and routines.

Figure 1.1 organises the strategy landscape into those three phases. Read it as a maturity model: each phase builds capabilities the next one assumes. Initiating without a baseline audit produces guessing; incorporating without committee governance produces burnout; institutionalising without earlier experimentation produces empty policy. The figure also shows why whole-institution approaches outperform isolated projects — the phases deliberately mix curriculum, campus, community and governance actions so that progress in one function reinforces the others. International frameworks such as the Talloires Declaration (1990), the SDG Accord and UNESCO’s ESD for 2030 roadmap supply ready-made commitments that phase-one institutions can adopt to anchor their journey externally.

Strategy is also about sequencing under constraint. Institutions rarely enjoy spare money or consensus, so successful change agents pick visible, low-cost early wins — a campus garden, a student SDG audit, one redesigned course — whose success builds the political capital for harder structural moves. They also pair top-down levers (policy, budget lines, accreditation) with bottom-up energy (student societies, faculty champions, community partners). The dance between the two is a recurring theme you will meet again in Unit 3, when we examine why so many well-meant initiatives remain, in the language of the literature, “ islands of innovation in oceans of business-as-usual.”

1.5
SDG Skills and Competencies

If education for the SDGs is to change what learners do, it must be explicit about the capabilities it builds. The most influential answer comes from Wiek, Withycombe and Redman (2011), whose review for academic programme development identified five key competencies in sustainability. Systems-thinking competence is the ability to see structures, connections and feedback loops across domains — understanding, say, how food, energy, water and livelihoods interlock in a district. Anticipatory competence is futures literacy: imagining alternative scenarios, from business-as-usual to sustainable, and judging their consequences. Normative competence is the capacity to reason with values — to decide what ought to be, not merely what is, and to negotiate sustainability and justice explicitly.

Strategic competence turns vision into feasible action: designing interventions, anticipating barriers, sequencing change — exactly the craft practised in Topic 1.4. Interpersonal competence rounds the set out: sustainability work is done with others, across disciplines, cultures and power differences, so empathetic listening, collaboration and participatory leadership are not soft extras but core machinery. Later frameworks, including UNESCO’s (2017) eight competencies and the GreenComp European framework, rearrange and rename these abilities, but the Wiek quintet remains the reference skeleton that examination answers and programme designs alike can rely on.

For future teachers there is a crucial second layer: these competencies describe you as much as your students. A teacher cannot cultivate anticipatory competence in learners without exercising it personally — which is why the engagement activities ask you to rate yourself on the radar above at the start and end of the course. Competency development is slow, iterative and best evidenced through artefacts: lesson plans, issue maps, project reports. Collect yours deliberately; they become the portfolio that Examination boards and employers increasingly value over marks alone.

2.1
Apprising, Supporting, Networking, Convening — the HESD Approach and its Research Areas

No single university can transform higher education alone; the field therefore organises itself through deliberate community-building actions. The International Association of Universities (IAU), whose global surveys and iau-hesd.net portal anchor this syllabus, describes its strategy through four verbs. Apprising and supporting means keeping the worldwide higher education community informed — surveying institutions, publishing guidance, curating good practice — so that a rector in one country can learn in an afternoon what took pioneers elsewhere a decade. Building solidarity and networking goes further: regional associations, North–South partnerships and thematic clusters connect institutions so that a small college can stand on the same evidence base as a research giant, an expression of SDG 17 (Partnerships) operating inside the sector itself.

Conversing and convening supplies the third mechanism: IAU international conferences, regional symposia and advocacy at UNESCO bring diverse voices — students, staff, leaders, funders — into one room where norms are negotiated and commitments announced. Convening matters because sustainability change is as much a political process as a technical one; a shared forum legitimises bold local action. Finally, the approach for HESD depends on research that tests and refines practice. The syllabus asks you to know the main research areas: curriculum and pedagogy for ESD; campus operations and the ecological footprint of institutions; institutional policy, governance and leadership for sustainability; community engagement and university–society partnerships; assessment, indicators and benchmarking of ESD; and student learning outcomes and competencies. Engagement Task 2 asks you to list and localise these areas for your own context — a deceptively simple exercise that reveals which areas your institution has neglected.

2.2
The Role of University in Society

Universities hold a singular position in society: they are at once creators of knowledge, transmitters of culture and expertise, and institutions with a public mandate. Chankseliani and McCowan (2021) capture this dual character in their analysis of higher education and the SDGs: universities possess unmatched potential to advance sustainable development — through the professionals they graduate, the research they produce, and the legitimacy they lend to public debate — yet they also carry perils: reproducing social inequality through selective access, chasing global rankings that distract from local needs, and producing research disconnected from community priorities. A critical student of this course should hold both sides of that ledger in view.

Cortese (2003) pushed the argument further: because universities educate the decision-makers of every sector and model institutional behaviour for society to imitate, no institution on Earth has a greater lever for a sustainable future. The university’s roles can be grouped in four clusters. As an educator, it forms the competencies of future professionals and teachers. As a knowledge engine, it researches the problems and solutions of sustainability transitions. As a social actor — the “third mission” — it serves its region through extension, service learning, cultural life and honest public advice, acting as something like the conscience of its community. And as an economic and civic anchor, it is often a city’s largest employer and buyer, so its procurement, employment and investment choices ripple far beyond campus walls. Each role doubles as a sustainability channel — and each is auditable, which is precisely what Engagement Task 1 will ask of your institution.

Did You Know?

The Times Higher Education Impact Rankings, launched in 2019, was the first global league table to score universities not on prestige but on their measurable contribution to the SDGs — thousands of institutions from over a hundred countries now submit data on goals from poverty (SDG 1) to peace (SDG 16).

2.3
University Engagement with the SDGs

When a university decides to “engage with the SDGs,” what should it actually do on Monday morning? International guidance converges on a five-step cycle, shown in Figure 2.1: Commit, Map, Integrate, Partner, Evaluate. Commitment is a governance act — a council resolution or signed accord — not a slogan. Mapping inventories where teaching, research and operations already touch the goals (most discover they contribute far more than expected, but incoherently). Integration then deliberately links curricula to targets, steers research calls, and aligns investments. Partnership extends the work outward to government, industry, NGOs and communities, while Evaluation closes the loop with indicators and public reporting — the step institutions most often skip, and the reason many claims of “embedding the SDGs” remain unverifiable.

What does the global picture look like? The IAU’s 2016 global survey (van’t Land & Herzog, 2017) found that a clear majority of responding institutions had adopted some sustainability declaration or policy, with teaching and research the most common arenas of action and campus operations close behind — but with external partnerships and systematic evaluation trailing. Figure 2.2 sketches that pattern in illustrative proportions: curriculum leadership dominates, student initiatives trail. Use the chart critically: where would your institution sit? Which bar would be shortest? Notably, survey respondents also identified the same Missing middle this course keeps flagging — support and recognition for the staff who must actually deliver the change.

2.4
Creating an Enabling SDGs Learning & Teaching Environment

Transformative learning does not flourish in hostile soil. Before pedagogy comes environment: the physical, social and institutional conditions under which teachers and students meet. An enabling environment for SDG learning has at least five characteristics. It is physically and psychologically safe — clean, accessible classrooms and a climate where controversial questions (climate justice, consumption, caste and gender equity) can be discussed without ridicule or reprisal. It is participatory: seating, timetables and norms invite dialogue rather than monologue, because sustainability values cannot be transmitted through purely one-way talk. It is resourced — with materials, internet access, flexible spaces and, above all, with time for teachers to redesign courses rather than add content to overloaded ones.

It is also institutionally supported: leadership signals that sustainability work counts in workload models, promotions and awards, echoing the finding from the IAU surveys that lack of staff incentives is a leading brake on HESD. And it is assessment-aligned: if examinations reward recall of facts, students will ignore projects and portfolios no matter how inspiring the lectures. Environments are therefore designed, not wished into being. A practical test for any classroom you occupy: can every learner speak at least once per session? Do displays, data and greenery in the room itself model the goals? Does the assessment scheme let a student demonstrate action, not only memory? Where the answer is no, you have found your first change project — environments are also excellent sites for the engagement tasks, because they respond quickly to small interventions.

Did You Know?

Researchers who study “green schoolyards” report that simply adding plants, daylight and flexible seating measurably improves attention, wellbeing and pro-environmental behaviour — the environment itself teaches, which is exactly what education through the SDGs predicts.

2.5
Pedagogical Innovation and the SDGs

Education for the SDGs demands more than new content; it demands new ways of learning. Transmission lecturing can deliver the “about,” but competencies grow through methods that put learners in the position of agents. The literature and the syllabus converge on a recognizable family of innovations. Problem- and project-oriented learning (POPBL, studied for ESD by Yasin & Rahman, 2011) organises study around real, ill-structured sustainability problems — water logging in the college’s own lane, energy waste in the hostel — letting systems thinking and strategy develop together. Case-based learning brings policy dilemmas into the room; community service learning partners students with local organisations so that service and reflection interleave; living labs treat the campus as the experiment station; and digital, blended and flipped formats free contact time for dialogue and fieldwork while connecting learners to global data and peers.

3.1
Incorporating Sustainable Development in Curricula & Sustainable Futures: the Three Horizons Framework

Curriculum is where sustainability intentions meet timetable reality — and where they most often stall. Well-documented barriers recur worldwide: curriculum overload (every addition displaces something someone values), disciplinary silos (sustainability is everyone’s problem and therefore no department’s job), staff uncertainty (educators asked to teach what they were never taught), and assessment traditions that reward recall over action. Fernandez-Sanchez and colleagues (2014) catalogue these from a state-of-the-art review, while Lozano’s earlier work showed the same barriers operating at institutional scale. Naming barriers is not defeatism; it is strategy — each named barrier implies its own counter-move, from curriculum mapping to team-teaching to assessment redesign.

For orientation amid struggle, the Three Horizons framework (Curry & Hodgson, 2008), shown in Figure 3.1, is the syllabus’s chosen compass. H1 is the dominant, declining system: business-as-usual curricula, still powerful, increasingly misaligned with the world graduates enter. H3 is the vision pulling us forward: transformative, whole-institution education for sustainability. H2 is the energetic, contested zone of innovation where the future is actually built — the pilot course, the elective, the living lab — whose practitioners must survive inside H1 structures. The framework’s gift is emotional as much as analytical: it reframes failure (an H2 pilot cancelled by a timetable committee) as normal weather in transition, not proof the vision is wrong. Change agents learn to work the H1–H2 boundary deliberately: find hosts, gather evidence, tell stories, repeat.

3.2
Responsible Research and Innovation — the RADAR Framework

Universities shape sustainability not only through teaching but through what they choose to research and how. The RADAR framework (highlighted in Steele & Rickards, 2021) compresses responsible research and innovation into five memorable qualities, traced in Figure 3.2. Responsible research anticipates consequences and involves those affected from the start — beneficiaries, communities, future users. Attentive research watches what its innovations actually do once loose in the world, listening especially to marginalised voices that formal evaluations miss. Disruptive research dares to question inherited assumptions — growth, rankings, publish-or-perish — that quietly steer whole fields away from sustainability. Authentic research keeps its methods honest and its values visible; it refuses the cosmetic “sustainability” label on business-as-usual work. Finally, regenerative research aims to leave people and places better than it found them — designing outputs that renew ecosystems and communities rather than merely extracting data.

Notice how RADAR converts a vague virtue (“be responsible”) into five checkable habits — a research counterpart to the competency framework of Unit 1. For teacher-researchers, the framework also poses uncomfortable, productive questions about the education research we ourselves produce: Who set our research questions? Who benefits from our publications? Could a participant recognise themselves respectfully in our reporting? Would our findings regenerate the school we studied? Engagement Task 4, the critical curriculum analysis, will reuse RADAR as an analytical lens: a curriculum is itself a kind of research instrument into what a society values.

Did You Know?

The word “regenerative” has moved from ecology into design, agriculture — and now education. A regenerative campus aims to give back more clean energy, water and wellbeing than it consumes: net-positive, not merely “less bad.” Several universities worldwide now run net-positive buildings that export power to their neighbourhoods.

3.3
Approach of Higher Education Institutions to SDGs: Disciplinary, Structural and Dimensions

Ask ten institutions how they engage the SDGs and you will find three recurring logics. The disciplinary approach treats sustainability through existing subject silos: engineering programmes add a green-technology module, commerce programmes add environmental accounting, education programmes add an ESD paper (as this one does). Its strength is depth and fit with accreditation structures; its weakness is fragmentation — students can complete whole degrees meeting sustainability only as one examinable chapter, never seeing the connections Wiek’s systems-thinking competence demands.

The structural approach changes the organisation rather than the syllabus: interdisciplinary centres, cross-faculty electives, living-lab offices, sustainability committees, joint degrees and community-embedded campuses. Structures create the collaboration channels that disciplines cannot — but they can also become hollow shells if content and culture do not follow, producing the “sustainability office as alibi” pattern that critical observers note in several national systems. The dimensions approach organises engagement around the three dimensions of sustainability — environmental, social and economic — asking of every programme: where is the ecological dimension, the equity dimension, the livelihood dimension? It is a auditing lens more than a structure, and it usefully catches blind spots: the environmental sciences course with no social-justice thread, or the entrepreneurship course that never prices ecological cost.

3.4
Make Teaching Sustainable — Shifts that Teachers Want and Students Need

“Sustainable teaching” has a double meaning that this topic honours. It means teaching about sustainability — and it means teaching in a way that is itself sustainable: pacing, workload and wellbeing that a teacher can maintain for decades without burning out, delivering learning that lasts in students’ lives beyond the examination. The popular discussion of “mindset shifts” in sustainable teaching (see, for example, the ASCD discussion listed in the references) converges on a small set of movements: from coverage to mastery, from teacher performance to student agency, from isolated classrooms to collaborative practice, from content volume to competency depth, and from compliance to purpose. Each shift simultaneously serves learning and lightens the load — mastery frameworks, for instance, replace the endless re-teaching demanded by coverage races.

3.5
ESD Indicators and Underlying Challenges

How would you prove that a university is educating for sustainable development — beyond quoting its prospectus? That is the problem ESD indicators try to solve, and it is harder than it looks. Veidemane (2022) reviews the attempt to build internationally comparable ESD indicators for higher education rankings and finds progress alongside stubborn difficulties: ESD spans teaching, research, campus and community, so single-number scores oversimplify; data are self-reported, inviting inflation; definitions vary across countries and languages; and the deepest outcomes — changed values and agency — resist measurement entirely. Institutions therefore triangulate: policy indicators (does a sustainability policy exist, with budget and targets?), practice indicators (percentage of courses with SDG-mapped outcomes; campus energy, waste and procurement data; community hours), and outcome indicators (competency self-assessments, graduate destinations, project counts). Figure 3.3 shows the barrier side of the ledger in illustrative form.

Two challenges deserve special attention because they corrupt measurement itself. The first is greenwashing: indicators that reward paperwork invite paperwork — a policy filed, a course renamed — while practice stays untouched; good indicator sets therefore privilege artefacts that are hard to fake (audited energy data, assessed student projects) over declarations. The second is the institutionalisation trap described by Trechsel and colleagues (2018) from a Swiss case: once ESD is formalised into offices, reports and committees, the structures that protect it can also freeze it, defending yesterday’s achievements against tomorrow’s ambitions. The lesson is not to abandon indicators — without them Engagement Task 6 would be impossible — but to treat them as instruments: read trends, not snapshots; ask who prepared the data; and pair every number with a site visit, a classroom, a conversation.

Engagement Activities — Six Guided Tasks

The syllabus prescribes six engagement activities, and this section turns each into a small guided project with steps, an expected output and a pro tip. Work through them across the semester rather than in one burst: Tasks 1 and 3 pair naturally, Tasks 4 and 6 reward a second reading of Units 1 and 3, and Task 5 is best attempted while you are actually teaching. Keep every output in your field notebook — together they form a portfolio you can cite in examinations, discuss in interviews, and act on in your first job.

1Explore the Sustainable Processes of Your Institution

Walk your campus as a sustainability auditor, using the five functions of Table 1.2 as your checklist: teaching, research, operations, community engagement and governance.

Steps   (i) Obtain or reconstruct the institution’s sustainability policy, if any. (ii) Interview one staff member and one student about energy, waste and water practices. (iii) Photograph three practices that impress you and three that concern you.
Output   A one-page “campus sustainability snapshot”: one paragraph per function, with evidence.
Pro tip   Reuse your 24-hour SDG clock from Topic 1.2 — which goals does the campus itself advance or neglect?
2List the Research Areas of HESD

Consolidate the six research areas from Topic 2.1 and localise them: which are active in your institution, your state, and Indian higher education generally?

Steps   (i) List the six areas from memory first. (ii) Search one recent Indian example for each area (use the reference list as a starting point). (iii) Star the two areas with the weakest local presence and draft one research question for each.
Output   An annotated six-row table: area · example · local status · my research question.
Pro tip   Quality beats quantity: one well-chosen question per weak area can seed a dissertation topic.
3Find Out the Pedagogical Innovations of Your Institution

Hunt for the H2 innovations of Topic 3.1 already alive around you: projects, service learning, living labs, blended formats, co-created courses.

Steps   (i) Ask three teachers from different departments to name their most innovative recent course activity. (ii) Classify each against Table 2.2. (iii) Sit in on one session if permitted, and record which of the five competencies you saw students exercising.
Output   A short inventory: innovation · pedagogy type · competencies engaged · transferability to your subject.
Pro tip   frame interviews around stories (“tell me about a session that worked”) rather than jargon; you will get honest answers.
4Critically Analyse the Curricula for Sustainable Development

Take one full programme document (for example, a B.Ed. syllabus) and audit it as a critical curriculum analyst, using the about/for/through lens and RADAR.

Steps   (i) Mark every course outcome that touches sustainability, and label it about, for or through. (ii) Note silences: which dimensions (environment, equity, economy) are missing? (iii) Apply RADAR: whose interests does the curriculum serve; what agency does it enable?
Output   A two-page critique ending with three concrete, costed recommendations for curriculum committees.
Pro tip   Quote exact outcomes in evidence columns; committees act on quotations, not impressions.
5Evaluate Your Classroom Teaching-Learning Environment for the SDGs

Use the five characteristics of Topic 2.4 — safety, participation, resources, institutional support, assessment alignment — to evaluate a classroom you teach or observe.

Steps   (i) Rate each characteristic 1–5 and justify with an observation. (ii) Interview two learners about whether they feel heard and challenged on sustainability issues. (iii) Design one low-cost intervention and trial it in your next session.
Output   A before/after evaluation sheet with ratings, learner voices and the result of your intervention.
Pro tip   Change one variable only — single-variable interventions teach you something; simultaneous changes teach you nothing.
6List the ESD Indicators of Your Institution

Build the first draft of your institution’s ESD indicator set, organised into policy, practice and outcome indicators (Topic 3.5).

Steps   (i) Draft two indicators per type that could be measured this year with existing data. (ii) Check each against the greenwashing test: could it be faked by paperwork alone? (iii) Present the set to a faculty member and record their feedback.
Output   A six-row indicator table: name · type · data source · greenwashing risk · first measurement date.
Pro tip   Pair one “easy” indicator with one “hard” one (e.g., audited energy data + a competency portfolio sample) so the set resists gaming.
